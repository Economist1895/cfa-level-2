Analysis of Financial Institutions

In 30 seconds

  • Banks are systemically important (contagion) → heavily regulated. Assets are mostly financial (loans, securities), carried near fair value.
  • Basel III = minimum capital (% of RWA), minimum liquidity (LCR, 30-day stress) and stable funding (NSFR, 1-year horizon).
  • CAMELS: Capital, Asset quality, Management, Earnings, Liquidity, Sensitivity. Rated 1 (best) to 5 (worst); composite is judgement-weighted, not a simple mean.
  • CAMELS misses: government support, mission, culture, competition, off-BS items, segments, FX, risk factors.
  • Insurers earn premiums + float income. P&C: short, lumpy claims → combined ratio, loss reserves, liquid portfolio. L&H: long, predictable claims → riskier assets, lower capital, ALM duration.

01Why financial institutions differ

  • Systemic risk: impairment in one part of the system spreads to the whole economy. Contagion = shocks spreading across sectors/countries. Bigger, more interlinked bank → bigger impact.
  • Bank liabilities are mostly deposits (often government-insured, to prevent runs). Bank assets are loans & securities → credit, liquidity, market and interest rate risk.
  • Banks vs insurers: insurance has less cross-border business (reinsurance is the exception) and foreign branches must hold local assets against local liabilities → global commercial banks carry the most global systemic risk.

Asset quality

  • Composition, riskiest last: liquid assets (cash, central bank balances, reverse repos) → investments → loans (largest, most judgement).
  • Loans at amortised cost, net of the allowance. Securities: IFRS 9 amortised cost / FVOCI / FVPL. US GAAP equity → FV through NI; debt → HTM / AFS / trading.
  • Reverse repo = the bank lends against collateral (a loan asset). "Assets held for sale" ≠ "available for sale".
  • Unrealised losses aged > 12 months (esp. HTM, which hides FV) → possible unrecognised impairment.
  • Allowance should move with impaired and past-due assets. Avoid credit concentration.

03Management, earnings, liquidity, sensitivity

Management

  • Ability to exploit opportunities while controlling risk. Outsiders see only circumstantial evidence: independent board, separate CEO/chair, active risk committee, clean internal-control opinion, related-party terms.
  • These show an environment where good management can flourish, not proof of it. Overall performance is the best indicator.

Earnings

  • Quality = unbiased estimates + sustainable sources. Key estimates: loan-loss provisions, Level 3 fair values, goodwill impairment, DTA recognition, contingencies.
  • Sustainability ranking: net interest income & fee/service income (high quality) > trading income (most volatile). A rising trading share = falling quality. Volatile NII hints at excess rate risk.
  • Profits driven by falling provisions = low quality.
  • FV hierarchy: L1 quoted price, identical asset, active market; L2 observable inputs (similar assets, inactive markets, yield curves, implied vol); L3 unobservable model inputs → most judgement.
  • Derivatives: cash flow or net investment hedge → FV changes to OCI. Fair value hedge or free-standing → FV changes to NI (volatile; may need extra collateral).
F4

Net interest margin

NIM=Avg interest-earning assetsInt. income−Int. expense​

Banks borrow short, lend long (maturity transformation). A flatter or inverted curve raises funding costs → NIM falls. Average-balance analysis explains the past; period-end balances are a better start for forecasting.

Sensitivity to market risk

  • Mismatches in maturity, repricing frequency, reference rate or currency. Assets repricing faster than liabilities → NII rises when rates rise.
  • Rate sensitivity tables are static (structure fixed, no management action).
  • VaR (e.g. 99%, 1-day): good for trends within one bank, poor across banks (different assumptions); ignores lingering, multi-day shocks. Falling VaR = less market risk.
  • Trading revenue ÷ average daily VaR = reward per unit of risk.

04Beyond CAMELS

CAMELS is neither comprehensive nor integrated, and the letter order ≠ importance (capital and liquidity are equally important under Basel III).

Bank-specific

  • Government support: size ("too big to fail", SIFIs), health of the national banking system, government ownership (extra security; a post-crisis stake reduction can signal strength).
  • Mission: community bank tied to its local economy vs diversified global bank.
  • Culture red flags: losses from narrow, outsized bets; restatements from control failures; above-average equity pay (risk-taking, short-termism); slow provisioning then big write-downs.

Relevant to any company

  • Competitive environment: chasing market share at any cost = risk.
  • Off-balance-sheet: unconsolidated VIEs (check the reasons for non-consolidation), leases, benefit plans (falling rates or weak returns → cash drains; a falling net obligation is favourable), AUM (fee driver, not on BS).
  • Segments (is capital allocated well?), currency exposure (home-currency strength → negative CTA reduces capital), risk factors, Basel III disclosures.

05Property & casualty insurers

  • Short policies; claims variable and lumpy, mostly known within a year.
  • Distribution: direct writers (own staff → higher fixed costs) vs agency writers (commissions → variable costs).
  • Underwriting cycle: low combined ratio (hard market) → entrants cut prices → ratio rises (soft market) → exits, tighter underwriting → prices rise again.
  • Loss reserves: largest, most discretionary expense. Under-reserving → underpricing → possible insolvency. Longer-tail lines are harder to estimate. Downward revisions of prior-year reserves boost pretax income: conservative initial reserving, or a lever to manage earnings.
  • Net premiums written = direct written − ceded to reinsurers. Earned = the part of written premiums earned over the period's coverage.

Dividend ratio = a liquidity measure. Combined after dividends is stricter (adds the discretionary cash outlay).

  • Investments: conservative, liquid, mostly fixed income (little equity/real estate). Check concentration by type, maturity, credit, industry, geography, issuer.
  • Liquidity: FV hierarchy as a proxy. Level 1 most liquid; Level 2 less liquid but not illiquid; Level 3 implies illiquidity.
  • Capital: no global risk-based insurance standard (IAIS developing one); jurisdictional regimes (EU Solvency II, US NAIC RBC).

06Life & health insurers

  • Diversify across revenue sources, products, geography, channels, investments. Premium income is the more stable source, so more diversification can mean more volatile revenue.
  • Key estimates: future policyholder benefits (actuarial), capitalised acquisition costs amortised on expected profits, security valuation. Surrenders add expense.
  • Distortion: assets at market values vs liabilities at historical assumptions → rate changes swing earnings.
  • Profitability: ROA, ROE, operating margin, plus A.M. Best ratios: benefits paid ÷ (net premiums written + deposits); commissions & expenses ÷ (net premiums written + deposits).
  • Interest rate risk: compare asset vs liability duration.
  • Liquidity: liquid assets vs near-term obligations (benefits + surrenders); the current ratio doesn't apply (no current/non-current split). Junk bonds and real estate are less liquid.

07Exam traps

⚠️
  • Bank assets are loans & securities; deposits are liabilities.
  • "Minimum % of RWA funded with equity" = capital requirement, not liquidity or stable funding.
  • Competitive environment (and government support, culture, off-BS) is outside CAMELS; M and S are in it.
  • Total capital ratio flat but CET1/Tier 1 falling → capital adequacy declined. CET1/T1 up but total down → mixed.
  • Rising allowance can track past-due-but-not-impaired growth even while impaired assets fall. Falling allowance/NCO = cushion shrinking.
  • Efficiency of obtaining premiums = UW expense ratio; estimating risks = loss & LAE ratio; overall = combined (< 100% efficient).
  • L&H (not P&C) have predictable claims, lower capital needs, riskier investments, and RBC sensitive to rates.
  • Lower trading share of revenue = higher earnings quality. Free-standing derivatives cut → less earnings volatility.
  • VaR compares a bank to itself over time, not to peers. Revenue/VaR up = better reward per unit of risk.
  • Yield-curve inversion → NIM decreases for borrow-short/lend-long banks. 30-day reverse repos affect the LCR.
