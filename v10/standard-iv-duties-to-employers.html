Standard IV: Duties to Employers

In 30 seconds

  • IV(A): act for the employer's benefit in job-related matters: no competing, no taking records, no soliciting clients before you leave.
  • Preparing to compete is fine on your own time. After leaving you may contact former clients using public info, not firm records.
  • IV(B): any outside compensation linked to your job or that could conflict needs written consent from all parties.
  • IV(C): anyone with staff under their control or influence must make reasonable efforts to prevent and detect violations through an adequate compliance system, and must investigate fully when one surfaces.

01Loyalty

IV(A)

Loyalty

In employment matters, act for the employer's benefit; don't deprive it of your skills and abilities, divulge confidential information or otherwise cause it harm.

  • Follow employer policies unless they conflict with law or the Code. Not a blanket rule to put the employer before personal and family obligations.
  • Applies to employees (including unpaid ones who gain experience and use firm resources). Independent contractors are bound by their agreement.
  • Independent practice: allowed, but to offer services the employer provides you must first notify it (services, duration, pay) and get consent to all terms.
  • Employees bidding on the same RFP as the employer = competing → violation without permission.
  • Recommending a stock your firm may acquire harms the employer (it can raise the price).

Leaving an employer

  • Duty lasts until employment ends (pay and responsibilities stop); resigning isn't the end.
  • Allowed: preparing on your own time (e.g. registering the new business with regulators); telling your clients you're leaving and the name of the new firm, after giving notice and following firm policy.
  • Not allowed before leaving: soliciting clients or prospects (even implicitly), giving new contact details or describing the new firm's services without permission, disparaging the employer, misusing trade secrets or confidential info, self-dealing.
  • "It's in the clients' interest" is no excuse. If the firm's conduct is egregious: resign first, then alert clients.
  • Records, models and client lists are firm property (including on personal devices): return or erase them, and take nothing without written permission. Work you built at the firm belongs to the firm.
  • Skills, experience and knowing former clients' names aren't confidential. After leaving, contact them using public sources, subject to any non-solicit agreement.
  • Social media: follow firm policy on departure notices. Updating your profile after you've left isn't solicitation. Firm-approved business accounts are firm assets; keep personal and business accounts separate.
  • Posting firm research on a personal blog leaks confidential info.
💡
Whistle-blowing: client and market integrity come before employer interests, so breaking policy or copying records can be justified only to protect clients or markets, never for personal gain.

02Additional Compensation Arrangements

IV(B)

Additional Compensation Arrangements

Don't accept gifts, benefits, compensation or consideration that compete with or could reasonably create a conflict with the employer's interest, unless you have written consent from all parties involved.

  • Includes direct pay from clients (e.g. a client's performance bonus) and indirect third-party benefits.
  • Written = anything documentable, email included.
  • Disclose through the supervisor or compliance: nature, approximate amount and duration. The payer should confirm the details.
  • A client bonus is fine if disclosed and approved in advance, despite the risk of favouring that account.
  • Getting approval first, declining paid travel and disclosing a dinner afterwards → compliant.
  • A private gift unrelated to work (from a sibling) doesn't conflict.
  • Part-time or contract staff: agree upfront on serving competing employers.

03Responsibilities of Supervisors

IV(C)

Responsibilities of Supervisors

Make reasonable efforts to ensure that anyone under your supervision or authority complies with laws, rules, regulations and the Code.

  • Covers anyone under your control or influence, not only direct reports, and whether or not they are CFA members.
  • You may delegate supervision, but you stay responsible for instructing delegates.
  • Minimum: an effective compliance system, which is more than a code of ethics: policies, monitoring and enforcement, in place before violations happen and sized to the firm.
  • No authority over firm policy? Work with superiors; flag inadequate systems to senior management. If you can't supervise properly, decline the supervisory role until procedures are fixed.
  • Missing a violation despite reasonable procedures isn't a violation. Knowing (or should know) that procedures aren't followed is.

When a violation surfaces

  • Respond promptly; conduct a thorough investigation of its scope.
  • The employee's assurances, a warning, or just reporting it to compliance are not enough.
  • Meanwhile limit or increase monitoring of the employee; afterwards review procedures.

Compliance practices

  • Code of ethics: plain-language principles, kept separate from detailed procedures; share it with clients.
  • Adequate procedures: clearly written and tailored; a compliance officer with authority and resources; supervision hierarchy; checks and balances; documented monitoring; permissible conduct; reporting and sanctions.
  • Disseminate, update and remind; add conduct to performance reviews; regular training.
  • Incentives that reward how results are achieved, not just revenue ("succeed at all costs" cultures breed violations).

04Exam traps

⚠️
  • A firm policy covering only officers and their direct reports is too narrow for IV(C).
  • Downloading a model a colleague built for the firm → IV(A) (firm property); passing it off as yours → I(C).
  • Independent practice isn't banned; it needs employer consent.
  • A job offer that requires bringing clients → soliciting before leaving is a violation, even with disclosure.
  • IV(B) consent: written, from all parties, obtained before accepting.
  • Contacting former clients after leaving is fine, but not using lists taken from the old firm.
  • The supervisor who didn't enforce existing personal-trading reporting rules → IV(C).
  • Same broker for personal and client accounts isn't banned; disclose it to the employer.
  • Supervisor told of misconduct: investigate and restrict; accepting assurances isn't enough.
