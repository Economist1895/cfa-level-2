Ethical and Professional Standards

Application of the Code and Standards: Level II

In 30 seconds

  • Seven case vignettes test one skill: spot which standard is (or isn't) breached, by whom, and the right fix.
  • Use the framework: Identify → Consider → Decide & act → Reflect.
  • Most-tested themes: fair dealing (pre-release tips, selective offers), conflicts and referrals, stricter local law, model diligence, process changes, MNPI from insiders.
  • Many items hinge on "not a violation": premium service, roadshow invites, mosaic, own-time models, properly labelled backtests.
  • Later cases ask for corrective action plus a firm policy statement.

01Ethical decision-making framework

StepAsk
IdentifyRelevant facts, stakeholders and duties owed, ethical principles, conflicts of interest.
ConsiderSituational influences (pressure, incentives), extra guidance (compliance, Code), alternative actions.
Decide & actChoose and carry out the action.
ReflectWas the outcome as expected? Why or why not? (Always ends the process.)
  • The steps needn't be in order, but reflection comes last.
  • Vignette routine: list each person → each act → map it to a standard → check the exceptions (disclosed? consented? public? own time?).

02Case lessons: clients & research

  • Premium service offered only to clients you think can afford it → III(B). Service tiers must be disclosed and open to all. Charging more for more service is fine.
  • Tipping a client about a strong buy before release, or building the position in that client's account early → III(B). A member who knowingly goes along with it → I(A) (participating in a violation). No personal trade → no VI(B).
  • Calling the biggest clients after the change is posted publicly → fine. Inviting large clients to an IPO roadshow → fine if others' IPO access is unaffected.
  • Different models for institutional and retail clients → fine (suitability differs).
  • Recommending one speculative asset at the same % for every client → III(C). Selling your own scarce holdings only to the largest clients → III(B); offer to all and allocate pro-rata, or don't sell.
  • Removing caveats from others' forecasts in your report → I(C).
  • Publishing research on a firm in which the owner's family has a stake, without prominent disclosure → VI(A).
  • Trading through the broker a colleague suggests instead of seeking best execution → III(A).
  • Sharing client data with a partner firm without client approval (written recommended) → III(E). Traders' screens visible to the prop desk → III(E), and the supervisor → IV(C).

03Case lessons: law, employer & conflicts

  • Multi-jurisdiction: applicable law (e.g. the clients' country) bans social media or instant messaging for investment info → stricter law wins → I(A). The supervisor who allowed it → IV(C). "Not required in other countries" is irrelevant.
  • Referral fees paid to and received from lawyers or partner firms, undisclosed → VI(C). Disclosure must come before the client signs.
  • Client-paid perks (bonuses, extras) without the employer's written consent → IV(B), even if documented in the IPS.
  • Donations to a referrer's pet charity, or a sub-adviser hired for political ties → I(B) (offering inducements).
  • Issuer-paid airfare and hotel → pay your own (I(B)); hosted meals and entertainment at the event → OK.
  • Using a rival's confidential papers found by chance (e.g. in a recycling bin) → I(D) dishonesty.
  • Mining or selling a crypto asset you recommend → VI(A) conflict (avoid or disclose to clients and employer) + IV(B) if it competes with the employer.
  • Pressure from founders to loosen due diligence → I(B). Hyping a listing with misleading claims → II(B). Marketing to minors without checking the law → I(A).

04Case lessons: models, process & performance

  • Reviewing a model's components on different schedules (parts quarterly, the optimiser annually) → V(A). Test every component and their interaction.
  • Knowing about a model error and waiting to fix it → III(A). If the boss refuses, go to senior management before dissociating or contacting clients.
  • Blaming underperformance on "market volatility" when the real cause was a disabled risk model → I(C).
  • A model built on own time, unpaid, used only for personal and family money → no IV(A) breach.
  • Showing actual and backtested results, clearly labelled, with a "past performance" caveat → no III(D) breach.
  • Prior-firm track record without naming the firm and your role → III(D) (and needs written permission to take the records).
  • Process changes: a new mandate or selection method → disclose to current and prospective clients (V(B)). A change still within the stated mandate (e.g. more countries but still within limits) → no disclosure needed.
  • Crypto recommended while still learning it → I(E). No written analysis of risks and benefits → V(A).

05Case lessons: MNPI, social media & CFA references

  • A specific, factual tip from a company insider (e.g. the CFO hinting at an asset sale) is material even before the board votes or the deal is final → trading on it = II(A). A model revised on the back of the tip doesn't help.
  • Connecting with clients on personal social media with the firm's permission, and announcing departure only after leaving → no IV(A) breach.
  • A small gift that isn't a referral fee or a conflict needs no disclosure.
  • Posting "the ethics questions were super hard" → fine; describing specific exam questions → VII(A).
  • Calling a Level III passer without the work experience "our newest CFA" → VII(B).
💡
Policy-statement pattern: state the rule (e.g. "disclose all outside compensation and get written approval first"), the procedure (approved list, investment committee review, pre-clearance) and the consequence (discipline up to termination).

06Exam traps

⚠️
  • "Who violated?" Check both parties: the tipper (III(B)), a knowing participant (I(A)) and the supervisor (IV(C)) often join the main offender.
  • Disclosure fixes conflicts and referrals, but not unfair allocation or a breach of stricter law.
  • Premium service and roadshow invites are fine; selective pre-release information is not.
  • Paid travel: avoid it (pay your own); event meals are fine.
  • Model error: fix it now; escalate to senior management before dissociating.
  • Labelled backtests are fine; unlabelled or cherry-picked results are not.
  • Insider facts are material even if the deal isn't final.
  • A change within the existing mandate needs no notice; a mandate or process change goes to current and prospective clients.
