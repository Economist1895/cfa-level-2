Basics of Multiple Regression and Underlying Assumptions

In 30 seconds

  • Multiple regression explains one dependent variable Y with two or more independent variables. Uses: find relationships, test theories, forecast.
  • Each slope is a partial coefficient: the effect of a one-unit change in Xj​ holding the other X's constant.
  • Continuous Y → linear regression. Discrete (0/1) Y → logistic regression.
  • Five assumptions: linearity, homoskedasticity, independent errors, normal errors, independent X's (no exact linear relation).
  • Diagnostic plots reveal violations: scatterplot matrix, residuals vs predicted, residuals vs each X, normal Q-Q.

01Uses & the regression process

  • Uses: explain return drivers (e.g. factor models), predict distress, test a theory, forecast.
  • Step 1: is Y continuous? No → logistic regression. Yes → estimate the linear model.
  • Step 2: analyse residuals. Assumptions violated → adjust the model and re-estimate.
  • Step 3: examine goodness of fit. Overall fit not significant (e.g. no significant X's) → adjust and re-estimate.
  • Step 4: is it the best of the candidate models? Yes → use it for analysis/prediction.
💡
X's can be continuous (financial ratios) or discrete dummies (e.g. sector membership). Only the type of Y decides linear vs logistic.

03The five assumptions

AssumptionMeaning
1. LinearityY is linear in the X's
2. HomoskedasticityResidual variance is constant across observations
3. Independent errorsObservations independent → residuals uncorrelated
4. NormalityResiduals are normally distributed
5. Independent X's(a) X's not random; (b) no exact linear relation among X's
  • Exact linear relation among X's → model cannot be estimated.
  • Approximate relation (high pairwise correlation) → model can be estimated but interpretation is problematic (multicollinearity).
  • Simple regression had four assumptions; multiple regression adds #5 for the extra X's.

04Diagnostic plots

PlotDetects
Scatterplot matrix (pairs plot)Y vs X: linearity (curve = violation). X vs X: independence of X's. Also outliers.
Residuals vs predicted YHomoskedasticity & independence of errors (clusters/patterns = violation); outliers
Residuals vs each XRelationship with an X → misspecification; extreme X values that drive the estimates
Normal Q-Q plotNormality: standardised residuals should sit on the diagonal
  • Good residual plot: random cloud centred on 0, no direction, no fanning.
  • Q-Q: points peeling off the line beyond ±2 SD → fat tails (common in financial data). About 5% of a normal sample lies below −1.65 SD.
  • Two X's showing a flat line against each other (little correlation) is the desired outcome.
⚠️
A pairwise scatterplot shows variables in isolation. An X can look unrelated to Y yet be significant in the regression, because the regression captures the partial effect.

05Exam traps

⚠️
  • Interpret a slope "holding all other X's constant". An answer without that qualifier (or that adds the intercept to the slope) is wrong.
  • Continuous Y + several X's → multiple linear regression, not logistic or simple.
  • The intercept is Y when every X is zero, not "the average Y".
  • Curved Y-vs-X scatter → violates linearity, not independence of errors.
  • Assess homoskedasticity with residuals vs predicted values; the X-vs-X scatter is for independence of X's.
  • A pairwise scatterplot detects linear relationships, not heteroskedasticity or normality. The Q-Q plot is for normality.
  • Significant pairwise correlation between X's → possible violation of independence of independent variables.
  • Prediction: watch signs (minus × negative X) and units (whole % vs decimals).
