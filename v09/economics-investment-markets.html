Economics and Investment Markets

In 30 seconds

  • Every asset = PV of expected cash flows. The economy moves prices only via cash flows, the real default-free rate, expected inflation or risk premiums.
  • Discount rate is built up in layers: real rate l + expected inflation θ + inflation-uncertainty premium π + credit γ + equity κ + illiquidity φ.
  • Risk premium comes from negative covariance between an asset's payoff and the marginal utility of consumption. Assets that pay off in bad times earn low (even negative) premiums.
  • Real rates rise with trend GDP growth and with GDP growth volatility. Policy rates follow a Taylor rule.
  • In a downturn: policy rates cut, curve steepens, credit spreads widen (low-rated and cyclicals most), P/E and property values fall.

Additive discount rate is an approximation of the multiplicative form. Economic news affects price only through the numerator (cash-flow timing/size) or the denominator (rates, premiums).

  • Prices respond to news (surprises vs expectations), not to anticipated data. "Good" data can cut prices if the market expected better.
  • Sentiment works mainly through risk premiums (discount rate).
⚠️
Higher real GDP growth has an ambiguous effect on equity value: higher expected earnings (numerator ↑) but a higher real rate (denominator ↑).

02Real rates & the consumption hedge

F2

One-period real default-free bond

Pt,1​=Et​(m~t,1​)lt,1​=Pt,1​1−Pt,1​​=Et​(m~t,1​)1​−1​
m~
inter-temporal rate of substitution = MU of future consumption ÷ MU of consumption today (random)

Real rate is inversely related to E(m). In equilibrium every investor's E(m) = the bond price.

F3

Risky asset price = risk-neutral PV + covariance

Pt,s​=1+lt,1​Et​(P~t+1,s−1​)​+covt​(P~t+1,s−1​,m~t,1​)

First term = risk-neutral PV. Covariance = discount for risk. Zero for a one-period default-free bond (payoff certain). Non-zero even for a default-free 2-period bond, because its price at t + 1 is uncertain.

F4

Risk premium from covariance

rt,s​−lt,1​​=Pt,s​Et​(P~t+1,s−1​)​−(1+lt,1​)=−Pt,s​1+lt,1​​covt​(m~t,1​,P~t+1,s−1​)​

Most risky assets pay off in good times (low MU) → negative cov → lower price → positive premium. An asset paying off in bad times → positive cov → negative premium, return below the risk-free rate.

  • Risk aversion = diminishing marginal utility: a loss hurts more than an equal gain helps.
  • Decreasing absolute risk aversion: wealthier investors have lower MU → demand a lower premium, buy more risky assets.
  • More income uncertainty → everyone saves more → lower bond price → higher required return.
  • Real default-free rate is positively related to trend real GDP growth (more future goods → less need to save) and to GDP growth volatility. Fast-growing EMs → higher real rates.
  • Best proxy for real rates: default-free index-linked (inflation-linked) govt bonds. Their price moves only with lt,s​.
  • Short-dated linkers can trade at negative real yields: safe-haven demand, limited supply, inflation fears.

03Short rates & the Taylor rule

  • Short nominal rate ≈ real rate + expected inflation (l+θ). Tracks inflation and the policy rate closely.
F5

Taylor rule

prt​​=lt​+ιt​+0.5(ιt​−ιt∗​)+0.5(Yt​−Yt∗​)=lt​+1.5ιt​−0.5ιt∗​+0.5(Yt​−Yt∗​)​
ιt​, ιt∗​
actual and target inflation
Yt​−Yt∗​
output gap (log actual − log potential GDP, ≈ %)

Neutral rate = lt​+ιt∗​ (inflation on target, zero gap). Inflation above target or a positive gap → policy rate above neutral. Net weight on inflation 1.5 vs 0.5 on output.

  • Positive output gap = producing above sustainable capacity → high/rising inflation. Negative gap → high unemployment.
  • Neutral rate shifts with trend growth, growth volatility and the inflation target.
  • Policy errors amplify cycles: too low for too long → credit bubble; too high → recession.

04Nominal bonds, BEI & the yield curve

F6

Default-free nominal bond

Pti​=s=1∑N​(1+lt,s​+θt,s​+πt,s​)sCFt+si​​

Nominal CF is certain; its real value isn't → premium π for inflation uncertainty, rising with maturity. Implied one-year premium: π=CF/P−(1+l+θ).

F7

Break-even inflation rate

BEI=ynominal​−yreal​=θt,s​+πt,s​​

Zero-coupon, default-free, same maturity. BEI is not pure expected inflation: with π > 0, expected inflation < BEI. If θ rises while π falls, the BEI move is indeterminate.

Shape over the cycle

  • Curve moves: level (≈ 93–95% of variation), slope, curvature (tiny).
  • Recession: central bank cuts short rates; long rates fall less (expected to normalise) → curve steepens.
  • Late expansion: inflation and short rates peak; long yields reflect expected lower inflation and weaker credit demand → curve flattens/inverts → inversion often predicts recession.
  • Inverted curve: mainly expected rate cuts. Upward slope is ambiguous: expected hikes, unchanged rates + term premium, or even cuts more than offset by the term premium.
  • Splitting a curve shift into real and BEI parts assumes the risk premium is zero or constant, which it isn't.

Bond risk premium (term premium)

  • Positive on average and rises with maturity → "normal" curve is upward sloping.
  • Govt bond returns are mostly negatively correlated with GDP → partial consumption hedge → modest premium.
  • Short-dated bonds are the better hedge (more negatively correlated with bad times) → investors accept lower returns on them.
  • Estimate: nominal yield − linker yield − survey expected inflation. Falls in crises (flight to the hedge); rises when the hedge is valued less. Estimates can go negative.
  • Other factors: supply/demand (fiscal surpluses, foreign central-bank buying), regulation/accounting (pension funds forced into long gilts → inverted long end).

05Credit premiums

F8

Credit-risky bond

Pti​=s=1∑N​(1+lt,s​+θt,s​+πt,s​+γt,si​)sEt​[CFt+si​]​

Implied one-year credit premium: γ=100/P−(1+l+θ+π). Measured credit spread (corporate − govt yield, same currency/maturity) ≈ but ≠ γ.

F9

Expected loss

EL=PD×(1−Recovery rate)

Risk-neutral investors would want only EL. Risk-averse investors want more, because defaults cluster in downturns → not diversifiable.

  • Spreads widen into and during recessions, narrow in recovery. Recovery rates are pro-cyclical; loss rates counter-cyclical.
  • Recovery ranking: senior secured > senior unsecured > subordinated.
  • Widening: low-rated and cyclical-sector spreads widen most → high quality outperforms (flight to quality).
  • Narrowing: investors less discerning → lower-rated outperform higher-rated, and corporates beat governments.
  • Company factors: profitability, interest coverage, FCF/debt, leverage.
  • Sovereigns carry credit premiums too (EM defaults despite printing power; eurozone crisis broke "default-free").

06Equities & the ERP

F10

Equity pricing

Pti​=s=1∑∞​(1+lt,s​+θt,s​+πt,s​+λt,si​)sEt​[CFt+si​]​λt,si​=γt,si​+κt,si​​
κ
premium of equity over the firm's own debt (residual claim, wiped out in bankruptcy)
λ
ERP over default-free govt bonds

ERP > credit premium because dividend size and timing are uncertain. The two premiums are positively correlated and both rise in bad times.

  • Equities are a poor consumption hedge (crash in recessions) → ERP positive. Ex post ERP ≈ 2–5% p.a. by country.
  • Cyclical (durables, discretionary) earnings fall hardest in recessions and rebound fastest. Defensive (staples) are stable.

What justifies a higher P/E

  • Higher expected real earnings growth; lower real rates (e.g. lower GDP volatility); lower expected inflation (θ); lower inflation uncertainty (π); lower ERP (λ); lower operating/financial risk.
  • P/E rises in expansions, falls in recessions.
  • CAPE (Shiller) = real price ÷ 10-year average real earnings. Smooths cyclical earnings swings.

07Commercial real estate

F11

Property discount rate

1+lt,s​+θt,s​+πt,s​+γt,si​+κt,si​+ϕt,si​

Bond-like rent (credit quality = tenant), equity-like residual value at lease end (κ), plus an illiquidity premium (φ).

Tenant / leaseDiscount rate (1 + …)
Govt, inflation-indexed rent (real CFs)l+κ+ϕ
Govt, fixed nominal rentl+θ+π+κ+ϕ
Corporate, fixed nominal rentl+θ+π+γ+κ+ϕ
  • Shortcut: required return = tenant's bond yield + property risk premium + liquidity premium.
  • Rental income is stable, but capital values are highly pro-cyclical → poor hedge → premium closer to equities than to govt bonds.
  • Illiquidity means you may be unable to sell in bad times → reduces hedge value → φ. Key difference from public equity valuation.

08Exam traps

⚠️
  • Highest real short rate = highest real growth (nominal growth − inflation) and highest growth volatility. Don't rank on nominal growth.
  • BEI ≠ expected inflation: BEI = θ + π, so expected inflation is below the BEI.
  • Implied premiums: 100/P minus 1 plus every other component. For γ, include π; for π, exclude it.
  • Only changes in real rates (driven by growth level/volatility) move real default-free bond prices, not inflation.
  • Recession → curve steepens (short rates cut more). An inverted curve precedes recession.
  • Recession: Aaa outperforms B, defensives beat cyclicals. Spread-narrowing phase: low-rated outperform.
  • Risk-averse: cov(m, price) is negative. A negative premium (return < risk-free) is possible for an asset paying off in downturns.
  • Wealthier investor (e.g. extra annuity) → lower required premium, more risky assets.
  • Higher expected inflation lowers justified P/E; lower inflation uncertainty raises it.
  • Upward-sloping curve says nothing definite about future rate direction (term premium).
