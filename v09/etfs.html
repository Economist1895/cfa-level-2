Exchange-Traded Funds: Mechanics and Applications

In 30 seconds

  • Primary market (OTC): only authorised participants create/redeem shares, in kind, with the issuer. Secondary market (exchange): where investors trade.
  • AP arbitrage keeps price ≈ NAV: premium → create & sell; discount → buy & redeem.
  • In-kind redemptions make ETFs tax fair and tax efficient. APs' costs go into the bid–ask spread, so buy-and-hold holders don't pay for others' trading.
  • Cost of ownership = round-trip trading cost (fixed) + management fee × time. Trading costs dominate short holds; fees dominate long ones.
  • Risks: counterparty (ETNs, swaps), fund closure, investor misunderstanding (leveraged/inverse daily reset). Uses: cash equitisation, rebalancing, completion, transitions, core and tactical exposure, factors.

01Creation / redemption

  • Mutual fund: bought/sold from the fund at closing NAV for cash. ETF: trades intraday like a stock; shares created/redeemed continuously.
  • APs = large broker/dealers (often market makers) authorised by the issuer. The issuer publishes a daily creation basket, which also defines the intraday NAV.
  • Investor buying on the exchange: the issuer is not involved and receives no new money.
ETF price vs NAVAP arbitrage
Premium (price > NAV)Buy the basket, deliver it for new ETF shares (creation), sell ETF shares → ETF price ↓
Discount (price < NAV)Buy ETF shares, redeem for the basket, sell the securities → ETF price ↑
  • AP pays all trading costs plus a creation/redemption fee and recovers them through the bid–ask spread. Frequent traders bear their own costs → structure is fairer than a mutual fund.
  • Arbitrage gap widens when the basket is hard to trade simultaneously (foreign market closed) or illiquid (high yield): the AP bears market risk until both legs are done.
  • US: trades clear through NSCC (guarantor once cleared), DTC keeps the book, continuous net settlement, T+2. Market makers get up to 6 days.
  • Europe: mostly institutional, OTC-negotiated, cross-listed, many CSDs → fragmentation can widen spreads.

02Expenses & tracking

  • ETF fees are usually lower: no individual investor accounts (held via brokers), mostly index-based (no research).
  • Tracking error = annualised standard deviation of daily return differences (fund NAV − index). Shows variability only, not whether the fund lags or leads.
  • Tracking difference = rolling 12-month return gap. Shows the cumulative outcome; compare its median with the expense ratio. Expect underperformance ≈ expense ratio.
  • Always report a central tendency (mean/median) and a spread (SD/range).

Sources of tracking error

  • Fees & expenses: index assumes frictionless trading at closing prices.
  • Representative sampling/optimisation (holding a subset): more tracking error than full replication, wider range, regime-dependent.
  • Depositary receipts / other ETFs: different trading hours; ETFs held at market price, not NAV.
  • Index changes traded at other times/prices. Usually minor: infrequent, and APs can deliver market-on-close.
  • Fund accounting: FX strike time (WM/Reuters 4 pm London vs ETF close), bond valuation time.
  • Regulatory & tax: dividend withholding rates differ from the index's; local taxes on foreign inflows.
  • Asset-manager operations: securities lending and dividend recapture are negative costs (help the fund vs the index).

03Tax issues

  • Tax fair: investor sales happen on the exchange; AP redemptions are in kind (not taxable) → remaining holders aren't hit by gains triggered by others. Mutual funds sell securities to pay redeemers.
  • Tax efficient: the manager delivers the lowest-cost-basis lots in redemptions → purges unrealised gains. Also lower turnover → fewer capital-gain distributions.
  • Gains distributions and taxes on sale are separate questions. On sale, ETFs are generally taxed like their underlying holdings.
  • Return of capital (paid beyond earnings): generally not taxed now; reduces cost basis.
  • Structure matters: e.g. in the US, commodity ETNs are taxed differently from futures-based ETFs.

04Spreads, premiums & total cost

F1

Bid–ask spread (upper bound)

Spread≤ ​± create/redeem & direct fees+ underlyings’ spreads+ risk of hedging/carrying+ market maker’s profit− offsetting-flow discount​

Main drivers: order flow (more volume → tighter), competition among market makers, and liquidity-provider costs/risks. Liquid futures on the index → easy hedging → tight spreads even for small-cap ETFs.

F2

Premium / discount

End of day=NAVETF price−NAV​Intraday=iNAVETF price−iNAV​​

iNAV = indicated intraday NAV from the creation basket.

  • Timing differences: foreign underlyings closed hours earlier; bonds priced at bid by pricing services → apparent premium. In stress, stale bond NAVs → apparent discount. The ETF price is then price discovery, not mispricing.
  • Stale pricing: infrequently traded ETF; last trade compared with a later NAV → large apparent premium/discount.
F3

Holding-period cost

Round trip=2×comm.+2×21​spreadCost=Round trip+fee×12months​​

Round-trip cost = full commissions both ways + one full spread. Trading costs are paid once; fees accrue with time → short-term traders care about spreads, long-term holders about expense ratio.

  • Other cost items: tracking error (±), premium (+) / discount (−) at trade, portfolio turnover inside the fund, taxes, securities lending income (−).

05ETF risks

  • ETNs: unsecured, unsubordinated bank debt promising the index return; no underlying holdings → highest counterparty risk (theoretically 100%). Monitor with the issuer's CDS. Deposit-based currency ETFs carry bank default risk too.
  • Settlement risk: swap-based (synthetic), leveraged and some active ETFs. Mark-to-market gains exposed between settlements (daily/weekly); collateral in T-bills.
  • Securities lending: over-collateralised (102% domestic, 105% international); low risk; income usually credited to holders.
  • Fund closure: regulation, competition (low AUM and volume are warning signs), corporate actions (issuer M&A). Liquidation can trigger capital gains.
  • Soft closures: creation halts (mostly ETNs; arbitrage breaks → large premium) and index/strategy changes.
  • Investor-related (expectation) risk: not understanding the exposure, e.g. leveraged/inverse funds.
F4

Leveraged / inverse daily reset

NAVt​=NAVt−1​(1+krindex,t​)Required exposure=k×NAVt​​

Daily objective only. Exposure is reset each day (a 3× fund adds exposure after gains). Over longer periods compounding means the return ≠ k × index return (e.g. index +10% then −10% = −1%; −2× fund: −20% then +20% = −4%, not +2%).

06ETFs in portfolio management

Efficient portfolio management

  • Liquidity management: cash equitisation (avoid cash drag); park small flows; hold for expected outflows.
  • Rebalancing: one liquid ETF trade; can short an ETF to cut exposure fast, then cover as underlyings are sold.
  • Completion: fill exposure gaps left by external managers without changing them.
  • Transition management: hold the benchmark ETF while firing/hiring managers; "fund" new managers with ETFs.
  • Very large owners may prefer SMAs: lower negotiated fees, customisation, and no public disclosure of large holdings.

Asset-class exposure

  • Core (strategic) exposure, incl. fixed income: bond ETFs are cheaper, more continuously priced and more liquid than trading bonds.
  • Tactical tilts: country, sector, duration, credit, currency, commodities; short selling. Thematic ETFs are narrow and volatile, so evaluate them like stocks.
  • For tactical trading, prefer the ETF with the highest trading volume in its category.

Active & factor investing

  • Factor (smart beta): rules-based screens/weights (value, quality, momentum, low vol, dividend). Usually long-term buy-and-hold, not tactical. Multi-factor → lower volatility, less timing upside.
  • Risk management: low-vol/beta ETFs, currency-hedged, duration-hedged credit, inverse ETFs for short-term macro hedges (less turnover in core holdings).
  • Alternatively weighted (equal, fundamental, dividend yield); discretionary active (mostly fixed income); liquid alts; dynamic multi-asset.
  • Unsuitable for ETFs: strategies needing secrecy (illiquid, concentrated, black box), and underlyings too illiquid for daily creation/redemption.

07Exam traps

⚠️
  • Premium → AP creates (buys basket, sells ETF). Discount → AP redeems. Don't flip them.
  • Only APs, in the primary market, create/redeem. Ordinary investor trades don't change shares outstanding.
  • Who pays transaction costs? The AP, passed on via the bid–ask spread to trading investors, not to all holders.
  • Tracking error = SD of daily differences (magnitude of deviation). Rolling tracking difference is better for judging the investor outcome.
  • Biggest tracking-error sources: sampling/optimisation and ADR holdings. Index changes are a minor source.
  • Holding-period cost: (m/12)×fee + round-trip commissions + one full spread. Don't annualise trading costs.
  • ETN = unsecured debt → greatest counterparty risk. Creation halts → ETN trades at a premium.
  • Leveraged/inverse ETFs hit their multiple daily only.
  • ETFs distribute fewer capital gains (in-kind redemptions, low turnover). Return of capital lowers cost basis and isn't taxed now.
  • Factor ETFs = strategic buy-and-hold; most-traded ETFs for tactical use.
