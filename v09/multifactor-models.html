Using Multifactor Models

In 30 seconds

  • APT: expected return = RF​ + Σ(factor premium × factor sensitivity). Weaker assumptions than CAPM; doesn't name the factors or how many.
  • Three model types: macroeconomic (factors = surprises), fundamental (factors = returns to attributes), statistical (factors = portfolios from return data; few assumptions, hard to interpret).
  • Active return = factor tilts + security selection. Active risk² = active factor risk + active specific risk.
  • IR = mean active return ÷ tracking error. Index fund IR ≈ 0.
  • Uses: return and risk attribution, index tracking (passive), alpha and alternative indexes (active), and strategic tilts to risks you have a comparative advantage in bearing.

01APT & arbitrage

F1

Multifactor return-generating process

Ri​=ai​​+bi1​F1​+…+biK​FK​+εi​​
bik​
sensitivity (factor beta / loading) of asset i to factor k
εi​

A time-series model of realised returns. Portfolio intercept and sensitivities = value-weighted averages of the holdings'.

F2

APT expected return

E(Rp​)=RF​​+λ1​βp,1​+…+λK​βp,K​​
λj​
factor risk premium (factor price): expected return on a pure factor portfolio (β = 1 to j, 0 to all others) minus RF​

Holds for well-diversified portfolios. No risk-free asset → replace RF​ with λ0​ (return on a zero-beta portfolio). One factor: two portfolios pin down RF​ and λ1​ (two points define the line).

APT assumptions (only three)

  • A factor model describes returns (number and identity of factors not specified; K ≪ number of assets).
  • Many assets → asset-specific risk can be diversified away.
  • No arbitrage among well-diversified portfolios.
  • CAPM by contrast needs mean–variance investors, one market factor, and a market portfolio. APT is the less restrictive equilibrium model.
💡
Finding arbitrage: combine two portfolios so the mix's β equals a third's (weights w, 1 − w). Same β but different E(R) → buy the higher-return one, short the lower, same money each side. Zero net investment, zero factor risk, positive expected profit. Buying pressure then removes the gap.
F3

Carhart four-factor model

Rp​−RF​=ap​​+bp1​RMRF+bp2​SMB+bp3​HML+bp4​WML+εp​​

market excess return
SMB

HML

WML

Fama–French 3-factor + momentum. CAPM "anomalies" (size, value, momentum) become priced systematic risks. E(ap​)=0 in equilibrium.

F4

Standardised sensitivity (fundamental)

bik​=σ(attributek​)attributeik​−attributek​​

Average stock → 0; one SD above average → +1. Puts all factors on one scale. Industry/country factors are dummies (1 or 0), not standardised.

  • Statistical models: factors are portfolios extracted from historical returns. Factor analysis → best reproduce return covariances. Principal components → best reproduce return variances. Minimal assumptions and easiest to apply across asset classes, but hard to interpret.
  • Fundamental factor groups: company fundamental (earnings growth, variability, leverage); company share-related (earnings yield, dividend yield, B/M, market cap, momentum, volatility); macroeconomic (industry membership, CAPM beta, yield-curve sensitivity).
  • Evidence: fundamental model explained far more of the return variance than a macro model, with industry the biggest factor. Adding macro factors added nothing. But macro factors are individually shown to be systematic, whereas industry exposure can be diversified away. Neither type is inherently superior.
  • Fixed income: macro (inflation, GDP surprises); fundamental/sector (duration, credit, currency, geography; e.g. short/intermediate/long govt, IG, HY, MBS, with weights from a constrained regression summing to 100%); style factors across asset classes (value, momentum, carry, volatility).

03Macroeconomic factor models

  • Surprise = actual − predicted. Predicted values are already in prices, so only surprises move returns.
  • Return = expected return ai​ + Σ bik​× surprise + εi​ (company-specific news).
  • bik​: a 1-point surprise in factor k adds bik​ points to return, holding other factors constant.
  • Factors work through expected cash flows or the discount rate, and are meant to be uncorrelated.
  • GDP-growth premium usually positive; inflation premium usually negative: assets that do well when inflation surprises upward are hedges, so investors accept a lower required return.
RegimeLikely winners
Low inflation, low growthCash, government bonds
High inflation, low growthInflation-linked bonds, commodities, infrastructure
Low inflation, high growthEquity, corporate debt
High inflation, high growthReal assets (real estate, timber, farmland, energy)
⚠️
Convert % surprises to decimals. If a vignette gives the surprises and asks for the portfolio's return, use intercept + Σ b × surprise. The intercept alone is the return only when all surprises are 0. To back out ai​: ai​=Ri​−∑bik​Fk​−εi​.

04Return attribution

F5

Active return decomposition

Active return=Rp​−RB​ =k=1∑K​(bp,k​−bB,k​)×Fk​+ Security selection​
bp,k​−bB,k​
active factor exposure (tilt), measured at the start of the period
Fk​
factor return over the period

First term = return from factor tilts. Residual = security selection (over/underweighting stocks that beat/lag).

  • Read the style off the exposures: positive HML → value; negative SMB → large-cap; positive WML → momentum/growth; negative WML → contrarian value.
  • Fundamental models are preferred for attribution: commonly understood terms, and style choices expressed directly.
  • A self-described "stock picker" whose active return comes mainly from factor tilts, with negative selection, got lucky on a factor bet. Ask whether they can forecast that factor.

05Active risk & IR

F6

Tracking error & information ratio

TE=s(Rp​−RB​) IR=s(Rp​−RB​)Rˉp​−RˉB​​

TE = active risk = tracking risk. Annualise: daily × 250​, monthly × 12​ (assumes no serial correlation). IR assumes the portfolio has the same systematic risk as the benchmark.

  • Typical TE: passive ≈ 0.1% or less; enhanced index ≈ 2%; diversified active large-cap 2–6%; aggressive 6–10%+.
  • Mandates can impose a minimum IR and a maximum TE versus the benchmark.

Work in variances (additive), then take the square root for TE. Each component's share = component ÷ active risk².

  • Active factor risk splits into industry and style pieces. Industry share ≈ 0 → industry-neutral manager.
  • Higher active specific share → more concentrated, less diversified stock bets.
  • Tiny total active risk → essentially passive.
  • A factor contributes to active risk whenever portfolio and benchmark sensitivities differ, even if the portfolio's own sensitivity to it is 0.

06Construction & strategy

  • Passive: match the index's factor exposures with a sample of its stocks.
  • Active: predict alpha or relative returns; set desired risk profiles.
  • Rules-based active / alternative indexes: transparent, low-cost tilts to size, value, quality, momentum, capturing "alpha" mechanically.
  • Pure factor portfolio (β = 1 to one factor, 0 to the rest): go long it to bet on that factor; go short it to hedge an existing positive exposure.
  • Long–short factor portfolio: buy the top 20%, short the bottom 20% ranked on the factor, equal-weighted, rebalanced monthly. Alternatives: rank by Pearson or Spearman rank IC.
  • Benchmark (equal-weight) multifactor vs risk parity (each factor contributes equal risk, using a rolling covariance matrix): RP gave lower return but much lower volatility, so a higher Sharpe ratio.
  • Rolling-window backtest: fit in-sample, test on the next out-of-sample period, roll forward. Using a second rolling window for the RP weights avoids look-ahead bias.
💡
Strategic decisions: CAPM says everyone holds the market + risk-free asset. Multifactor view: tilt toward priced risks you have a comparative advantage in bearing, and away from those you're exposed to elsewhere. Wealthy investor with no job risk → can overweight business-cycle risk (cyclicals, high yield). Salary-dependent investor → wants a premium for recession risk. Long-horizon endowment → can bear business-cycle and illiquidity risk, but may be hurt by inflation.

07Exam traps

⚠️
  • "Arbitrage opportunities exist" is not an APT assumption; no arbitrage is.
  • Arbitrage: match β with a weighted mix, then buy the higher-E(R) side and short the other. Check which side is cheap before choosing the strategy.
  • Macro factors are surprises (actual − forecast), not levels. A negative surprise × positive b lowers return.
  • Statistical models: minimal assumptions but harder to interpret. Claiming "easier to interpret" is wrong.
  • Fundamental: sensitivities first, factor returns estimated. Macro: factor series first, sensitivities estimated.
  • Multifactor models help both passive (index replication) and active management.
  • "Largest effect on returns" means compare each b × surprise in absolute value; "most sensitive to combined surprises" means sum them per fund.
  • Active return not from security selection → from factor tilts. Index fund that tracks well → IR ≈ 0.
  • Ways to raise IR: take active factor risk and/or active specific (selection) risk.
  • Style analysis of an equity manager → fundamental model is most direct.
