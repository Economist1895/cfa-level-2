Backtesting and Simulation

In 30 seconds

  • Backtesting asks how a strategy would have done in the past, to gauge its risk–return trade-off. It assumes the future resembles the past.
  • Three steps: strategy design → historical investment simulation (rolling window: train in-sample, test out-of-sample, roll) → output analysis.
  • Pitfalls: survivorship bias, look-ahead bias (reporting lags, revisions, vendor back-fill), data snooping.
  • Historical scenario analysis → performance by regime (recession, high volatility). Simulation (historical bootstrap or Monte Carlo) adds randomness. Sensitivity analysis changes the assumptions, e.g. normal → skewed t.
  • Factor returns are negatively skewed, fat-tailed and tail-dependent, so a normal distribution understates downside risk.

01Objectives & process

  • Goal: approximate the real investment process to see the risk–return trade-off. Used as an acceptance or rejection screen. Common with quant managers and also widely used by fundamental managers, e.g. to test a screening metric.
  • Step 1, strategy design: goal (e.g. excess return or risk-adjusted absolute return) → hypothesis (trading rule or selection criterion) → rules and key parameters.

02Rolling window & output

  • Step 2, historical simulation: at each rebalance date, form the portfolio from in-sample data, hold it over the next out-of-sample period, then roll forward. Many samples, not just two; today's OOS data join the next in-sample window. Mimics live investing as new information arrives.
  • Long–short hedged portfolio: rank the universe on the factor, split into quantiles (quintiles or deciles), go long the top and short the bottom, equal- or cap-weighted. Drawback: not every manager can short. Quantiles may differ in beta and other factor exposures.
  • Step 3, output: mean return, volatility, Sharpe, Sortino, maximum drawdown (largest peak-to-trough loss), VaR, CVaR, turnover. Visuals: return time series, return distribution vs the normal, cumulative return on a log scale (equal % moves = equal height).
  • Cumulative plots reveal downside risk, performance decay and structural breaks: recessions and crises, geopolitics and trade blocs, monetary/fiscal shifts (e.g. QE), technology shocks.
  • Excess kurtosis → more extreme surprises; negative skew → surprises are more likely losses. Risk-averse investors prefer positive skew and modest kurtosis.
⚠️
Max drawdown and SD are not substitutes: SD measures volatility, drawdown measures downside. You can't infer returns or turnover from risk metrics alone. Lower VaR, CVaR and drawdown → less downside risk.

03Backtesting multifactor strategies

  • Build long–short factor portfolios (e.g. value, growth, momentum, revisions, ROE, leverage, earnings quality), then combine them: Benchmark (BM) = equal weights; risk parity (RP) = equal risk contribution, using factor volatilities and correlations. Both are long-only across factors (weights ≥ 0, summing to 100%).
  • Rolling window runs twice: once to form the factor portfolios, again to estimate the covariance matrix (e.g. trailing 5 years) for the RP weights. The second window avoids look-ahead bias.
  • Typical finding: RP has a slightly lower return but much lower volatility → higher Sharpe, smaller drawdown, VaR and CVaR, and near-symmetric returns. BM shows negative skew and high kurtosis.
  • Equal weighting often does as well as sophisticated optimisation.

04Common backtesting problems

ProblemWhat & fix
Survivorship biasTesting only firms that exist today; failures, takeovers and delistings are dropped. Can even reverse conclusions. Fix: point-in-time constituents. It is a type of look-ahead bias.
Look-ahead biasUsing information not known at the time: reporting lags, restated or revised data, vendor back-filled history for newly added firms. Inflates results. Fix: point-in-time data or an assumed lag.
Data snooping (p-hacking)Test many models and pick the best: an inference made after seeing results → false positives. Fix: higher hurdle (t > 3.0) and cross-validation (other periods or other markets).
  • A uniform reporting lag fixes look-ahead bias but creates stale data for fast reporters (large caps report within ~1–2 months). A sensible lag is about 1–3 months depending on region; a 4–6-month lag is stale.
  • Point-in-time data fix survivorship and look-ahead bias but not data snooping.
  • Rolling-window backtesting is itself a form of cross-validation (deterministic, not random).

05Historical scenario analysis

  • A backtest split by regime to study structural breaks. Examples: expansion vs recession, high vs low volatility (VIX above or below its 5-year moving average), trade agreement vs none.
  • Compare Sharpe ratios by regime: a robust strategy keeps a high Sharpe in every regime. Density plots show how the shape (volatility, skew, tails) changes.
  • Recession dates are known only in hindsight, which is a look-ahead issue in regime labels.
  • Switching universes (one index → another) is not scenario analysis: there is no regime change.

06Simulation

  • Why: history happened only one way and isn't stationary. Simulation adds randomness to verify backtest results, especially downside risk.

Eight steps

  • 1 Target variable (strategy return); 2 key decision variables (asset or factor returns and weights); 3 number of trials N (typically 1,000–10,000); 4 distribution of the decision variables; 5 draw random numbers; 6 compute the target; 7 repeat N times; 8 compute metrics (Sharpe, CVaR, drawdown).

07Sensitivity analysis

  • Change an input assumption and re-run to see how the target and risk measures react. Classic case: replace the multivariate normal with a multivariate skewed Student's t to capture skew and fat tails.
  • Skewed t: better fit but more parameters → larger estimation error.
  • Typical result: Sharpe is fairly insensitive to the method. CVaR is sensitive: Monte Carlo (normal, and even skewed t) understated the BM strategy's tail risk compared with backtesting and historical simulation. RP, with near-symmetric returns, looked the same under every method.
  • If methods disagree on tail risk → try more distributional forms.
💡
Classify the action: change the distribution assumption → sensitivity analysis. Split the sample by regime → scenario analysis. Sample past months at random → historical simulation. Draw from a fitted distribution → Monte Carlo.

08Exam traps

⚠️
  • "Backtesting is used almost only by quant managers" is false; fundamental managers use it too.
  • "What if the future differs from the past?" is the core weakness of backtesting (and historical simulation). VaR and CVaR from a backtest don't fix it.
  • Client restricted to domestic securities → change the investment universe.
  • Smallest downside by "average of losses beyond the threshold" → lowest CVaR, not VaR or drawdown.
  • Choosing the best of thousands of backtests → data snooping.
  • A long (e.g. 4-month) uniform reporting lag → stale data, not snooping.
  • N trials > history length in historical simulation → bootstrap (sample with replacement), not interpolation.
  • Non-normal factor returns in Monte Carlo → run sensitivity analysis with another distribution.
  • Best risk-adjusted performance across regimes → highest Sharpe in each regime.
  • Rolling window: the data are not split into just two samples; RP needs the window twice.
