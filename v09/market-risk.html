Measuring and Managing Market Risk

In 30 seconds

  • VaR = the minimum loss expected a given % of the time over a given horizon. It is not the worst case.
  • Three VaR methods: parametric (normal, μ/σ/correlations), historical simulation (reprice today's holdings with past factor moves), Monte Carlo (simulate from any chosen distribution).
  • Extensions: CVaR (average loss beyond VaR), incremental VaR (VaR change from a trade), marginal VaR (small change; sums to total), ex ante TE (relative VaR).
  • Sensitivity measures (beta, duration/convexity, delta/gamma/vega) say how much value moves, not how likely the move is. Scenarios and stress tests cover what VaR misses.
  • Constraints: risk budgets, position limits, scenario limits, stop-losses, capital allocation. Leverage, risk mix and accounting/regulation drive which measures each participant uses.

01Value at risk

  • Three elements: loss size (currency or % of value), probability (5% VaR = 95% confidence), horizon.
  • Correct wording for a 5% daily VaR of X: "on 5% of days, a loss of at least X"; "95% of days, a loss of no more than X" (gains included). Daily 5% ≈ once a month (every ~20 trading days).
  • Higher confidence or a longer horizon → larger VaR.
  • First step for every method: risk decomposition, mapping holdings to risk factors (e.g. a foreign convertible = currency + equity + several yield-curve points). Then gather a lookback-period history.

02Three VaR methods

MethodStrengthsWeaknesses
Parametric (variance–covariance)Simple; needs only μ, σ, correlationsNormality understates tails; very sensitive to inputs (esp. covariances); poor with options (non-linear, truncated payoffs)
Historical simulationNo distribution assumed and no parameters estimated; uses what actually happened; handles optionsOnly valid if the future resembles the past; a short lookback that includes a crash overstates its frequency; equal weights unless recent data are weighted more
Monte CarloAny distribution; many factors; handles options and complex interactionsComplex, output depends on assumptions; no standard number of trials; must model correlations
  • Historical 5% VaR = 5th percentile of repriced returns (sorted worst → best), not 1.65σ below the mean.
  • Monte Carlo ≈ parametric when it uses the same normal distribution and parameters with a large number of trials.
  • Historical and Monte Carlo can scale daily data to annual returns and estimate an annual VaR, provided the distribution is stationary.
  • Choose Monte Carlo when you expect volatility unlike the lookback period, fat tails, many holdings, or a short return history.
  • No single right method; estimates will differ.

03VaR: pros, limits, extensions

Advantages

  • Simple and easy to communicate (one number)
  • Compares risk across asset classes and desks
  • Helps capital allocation and risk-adjusted performance
  • Verifiable by backtesting
  • Widely accepted by regulators (who don't prescribe method or maximum)

Limitations

  • Subjective: cutoff, horizon, method, inputs
  • Underestimates extreme left-tail events
  • Ignores liquidity
  • Correlation risk: correlations rise in stress
  • Vulnerable to trending (many sub-VaR losses) and low-volatility regimes
  • Misread as worst case; oversimplifies; ignores the right tail
ExtensionAnswers
Conditional VaR (expected shortfall, expected tail loss)Average loss given VaR is exceeded. Best from historical or Monte Carlo (average of the worst 5% of outcomes).
Incremental VaRVaR after − VaR before for a proposed trade, a new position or a change in size.
Marginal VaRVaR change for a very small change in a position. Weighted MVaRs sum to total VaR → risk contributions.
Ex ante tracking error (relative VaR)VaR of portfolio holdings minus benchmark holdings (benchmark entered as shorts). Usually 1-SD, annualised. Perfect match → ~0.

  • Equity: beta = cov(Ri​,RM​)/var(RM​); market beta = 1.

Delta
Δoption ÷ Δunderlying. Call 0 to 1, put 0 to −1. Deep ITM call → 1, OTM → 0.
Gamma
Δdelta ÷ Δunderlying (second-order, like convexity). Largest when at the money and near expiry.
Vega
Δoption ÷ Δvolatility (first-order; effect of the volatility level)

All are approximations. Portfolio Greeks = position-weighted sums. The underlying has delta 1 and no gamma or vega. To cut a long index position's delta, sell calls or buy puts.

  • Sensitivities show what drives VaR, but not the probability of a move. A 1 bp sensitivity doesn't distinguish high-yield from IG risk: HY spreads move more. Fix: measure the sensitivity to a one-SD move.
  • Granularity: positions in one bucket are assumed perfectly correlated (a 4-year short offsets a 2-year long). Broad buckets hide curve risk; narrow buckets add complexity.
  • Position size alone works only for homogeneous, long-only portfolios.

05Scenario analysis & stress tests

  • Scenario measures vs sensitivities: multiple factors moved at once, and by larger amounts.
  • Stress test = extreme negative shock to a particular exposure. Leveraged firms (banks, hedge funds) prefer single-factor, pass/fail stress tests of capital. Long-only managers are least likely to use them.
  • Historical scenario: replay a past crisis on today's holdings. Reprice bonds and derivatives through valuation models using historical yields, spreads, implied vols, not the instruments' own price histories (maturities change; new issues didn't exist). Map post-event securities to proxies or factors. Usually applied instantaneously, with no management action.
  • For options and embedded options, fully reprice. Delta/gamma and duration/convexity fail for large moves.
  • Hypothetical scenario: moves that haven't happened. Can stress correlations (correlations rise in stress; hedges decouple, e.g. bond vs CDS), liquidity and concentration. Reverse stress test: target the portfolio's largest exposures and ask what would hurt them all at once.
  • Scenarios complement VaR: no reliance on history, no normality assumption, can stress concentrated positions harder.
  • Limits: history won't repeat exactly; hypothetical co-movements and magnitudes may be wrong; hard to build and maintain; no probability attached, so limits are hard to set.
  • Use scenarios as the final screen, after position, exposure and VaR/TE limits, to understand risk rather than eliminate it.

06Risk constraints

  • The limit, not the confidence level, drives conservatism: a loose 99% limit can bind less than a tight 84% one.
  • Standalone limits at desk level waste diversification. Fix: over-allocate, or budget with marginal VaR so desk budgets sum to the total.
ConstraintWhat it does
Risk budgetingTotal risk appetite set at the top, allocated to units (VaR, ex ante TE budget, surplus at risk)
Position limitsCap market value or notional per issuer, country, currency, category, or vs trading volume. Controls concentration, which VaR handles poorly.
Scenario limitsMaximum loss under a scenario; set higher tolerance for more extreme scenarios
Stop-loss limitsCut or liquidate after a cumulative loss over a period; counters VaR's trending weakness. Dynamic version = drawdown control / portfolio insurance.
Capital allocationEconomic capital at very high confidence; compare return per unit of capital with a hurdle rate (higher % return wins, not higher absolute profit)

07Who uses which measures

  • Drivers: leverage, mix of risk factors, accounting/regulation. Leveraged → high-confidence, short-horizon tail measures. Unleveraged → lower confidence, longer horizons, focus on underperformance.
  • Banks: liquidity gap, VaR (trading book), leverage ratio, sensitivities incl. key-rate and credit-spread duration, Greeks, economic capital (99–99.99%, 1 year, whole balance sheet), stress tests and scenarios.
  • Traditional asset managers: relative measures in % of assets: ex ante TE, active share, position limits, liquidity, redemption risk. With a cash benchmark, VaR = ex ante TE.
  • Ex post TE (past holdings, past markets) judges skill. Ex ante TE (current holdings, past market variability) shows current risk at once after a strategy change.
  • Hedge funds: sensitivities, gross exposure, leverage, VaR (high confidence, short horizon, rarely relative), strategy-specific stress tests, maximum drawdown for non-normal strategies (credit, event, illiquid, option-selling, correlation-dependent).
  • DB pension funds: liability cash flows as short bond positions; surplus at risk = VaR of assets vs liabilities, usually 1 year → liability-driven investing; split into liability-hedging and return-generating assets.
  • P&C insurers: sensitivities vs target allocation, economic capital and VaR for catastrophic loss, scenarios stressing market and insurance risk together. No asset–liability matching (premiums pay claims). Life insurers: asset–liability mismatch; scenarios include longevity.

08Exam traps

⚠️
  • "95% of the time we lose less than X" is wrong (implies losses every day). Also wrong: "VaR is the maximum loss" or "the expected loss".
  • A 1% VaR is not a 1% fall; it's 2.33σ below the mean.
  • No VaR breaches yet a big cumulative loss → trending or lower volatility than the lookback, not the confidence level.
  • Annual VaR from daily inputs: annualise μ (×250) and σ (×√250) before applying z.
  • Future volatility unlike history, fat tails or short history → Monte Carlo, not historical simulation.
  • Tail-loss question → CVaR. Effect of a trade → incremental VaR. Assets vs liabilities → surplus at risk. Limit of X% per security → position limit.
  • VaR does not capture liquidity. Forward-looking + tail risk → CVaR, stress tests, scenarios.
  • Historical scenario for bonds: use historical yields, not prices or durations. Limitation: it assumes history repeats exactly.
  • Sovereign downgrade that hits all assets and liquidity → hypothetical scenario.
  • Delta is approximate; gamma is second-order and vega first-order (vol level). Lower a portfolio's delta → short calls.
