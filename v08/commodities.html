Introduction to Commodities and Commodity Derivatives

In 30 seconds

  • Commodities are physical assets with no cash flows (they cost money to store). Value = discounted forecast of future prices driven by supply & demand, not DCF.
  • Backwardation: spot > futures (near > far). Contango: spot < futures. Curves can flip between the two.
  • Three theories of the curve: insurance (backwardation), hedging pressure (either, depending on who hedges more), storage (F = S + storage − convenience yield).
  • Fully collateralised futures return = price + roll + collateral. Roll return is positive in backwardation, negative in contango.
  • Swaps (excess return, total return, basis, variance/volatility) and indexes differ by weighting, roll and rebalancing rules.

01Sectors & life cycles

  • Fundamental analysis = supply & demand: stocks (capacity: land, fields, mines, herds) and flows (use of that capacity), plus timing (seasonality, shocks) and money flow (sentiment, rates, subsidies).
  • Data are messy: non-normal returns, shifting correlations, non-stationary coefficients.

Life cycles

  • Short life cycle → supply adjusts fast. Long life cycle → slow reaction to shocks.
  • Energy: natural gas is usable almost immediately after extraction. Crude must be refined ("cracking"). Both are stored. Refineries are costly; pipelines are often the casualty in conflicts.
  • Metals: most flexible (store for years, no spoilage). Big economies of scale in smelting → producers find it hard to cut output when supply > demand. Variability comes mostly from demand.
  • Livestock: poultry weeks, hogs months, cattle years (feeder → live cattle). Historically the least export-oriented because of spoilage; freezing tech is changing that.
  • Grains: plant → grow → pod/ear → harvest. Seasons are opposite in the two hemispheres. Stored year-round in silos.
  • Softs: coffee harvested somewhere all year near the Equator. Arabica (NY) and robusta (London) contracts are on green beans and are not interchangeable.

02Valuation & market participants

  • Stocks and bonds are claims on cash flows. Commodities generate none; owning them costs storage and transport. Some (electricity, weather) aren't even physical.
  • Most investment is via derivatives (futures) with finite lives.
  • Not everyone can make or take delivery → arbitrage gives a range (bounds) linking spot and futures, not an exact equality.
ParticipantRole

Hedging ≠ long, speculating ≠ short. Food maker long corn = hedge; miner short gold = hedge; oil major betting on its view = speculation; CTA short = speculation.

03Spot, futures & curve shape

F1

Basis & calendar spread

Basis=Spot−FuturesCalendar spread=Fnear​−Ffar​​

Positive → backwardation (spot > near > far). Negative → contango. Spreads trade as their own instrument.

  • Spot prices are local (location, quality). Futures are standardised and global/regional, with premiums or discounts for delivery grades.
  • Physical delivery forces spot–futures convergence. Cash-settled contracts may not fully converge and are worthless after expiry. Cash settlement drew in speculators and arbitrageurs.
  • Shape reflects both structural factors and expected supply/demand, and can flip quickly under stress.

04Theories of futures returns

TheoryIdea → curve
Insurance (normal backwardation)Producers sell forward to lock in revenue and accept a discount. Speculators earn a premium → backwardation. Evidence weak: "normal backwardation is not normal"
Hedging pressureProducers hedge more → backwardation. Consumers hedge more → bid up futures → contango. Balanced → flat. Hard to measure the imbalance
StorageInventory level drives the curve. Stored commodity (supply dominates) → contango. Just-in-time use, scarce stock (demand dominates) → backwardation
F2

Theory of storage

F=S+Storage−Conv. yield
Storage
Direct costs: rent, insurance, inspection, spoilage
Conv. yield
Benefit of holding physical stock as a buffer against disruption

Convenience yield is high when inventories are scarce and low when stock is abundant. Conv. yield > storage → backwardation. Both inputs are largely unobservable.

💡
Only hedging pressure can explain contango driven by consumers. Insurance theory always predicts backwardation.

05Components of futures returns

F3

Total return (fully collateralised)

TR=Price+Roll+CollateralPrice return=Pprev​Pnow​−Pprev​​​

"Fully collateralised" = cash equal to the futures' notional, so no leverage. An index can add a rebalance return.

F4

Roll return

Roll=Fnear​Fnear​−Ffar​​×%rolled

Backwardation (near > far) → positive. Contango → negative. Flat → zero. Multiply by the share of the position rolled that day (e.g. a 5-day roll = 20%/day).

F5

Collateral return

Collateral=rf​×12months held​

Usually T-bill yield. For long-horizon forecasts use a bond matching the horizon.

  • Rolling to hold exposure constant: contracts = exposure / price. Backwardation (far cheaper) → buy more far contracts. Contango → buy fewer.
  • Roll return is an accounting split, not a stand-alone return you can capture.
  • Long history (broad index): roll return small and negative on average; collateral return was the biggest piece; volatility comes almost entirely from price return.
  • Roll return matters in any single period but is modest over many. Its correlation with price return is ≈ 0.
  • By sector: stored commodities (metals, ags, livestock, softs, gold) → negative roll. Only energy (ex-nat gas) has a chance of a positive roll.

06Commodity swaps

  • Why swaps: one contract instead of managing many futures, plus customisation (specific grade, blend, dates). Dealer hedges with futures, physical deals or another swap, or keeps the risk.

Excess returnPays the change in the futures/index price (or the amount above a fixed level) × notional. Paying above a fixed price for a premium ≈ a strip of calls
Total returnIndex change incl. collateral interest. Index up → buyer (long) receives; index down → buyer pays. Settled each reset
BasisDifference between two related, imperfectly correlated prices (liquid vs illiquid grade)
Variance / volatilityRealised vs fixed variance (vol). Realised > strike → buyer receives. Seller profits if realised is lower. A bet on volatility, not direction

07Commodity indexes

  • Uses: benchmark, macro indicator, basis for investment products. Must be investable.
  • Differences: breadth, weighting, roll method, rebalancing frequency, governance (rules-based vs committee-selected).

  • Frequent rebalancing wins in mean-reverting markets and loses in trending ones (sells winners that keep rising).
  • Production weighting is self-rebalancing in part (weights float with prices).
  • Indexes holding commodities usually in backwardation earn a positive roll → better returns. One in contango lags.
  • Indexes correlate highly with each other (>70%) and ≈ 0 with stocks and bonds. No index dominates.

08Exam traps

⚠️
  • Calendar spread sign: near − far. A negative spread = contango, not backwardation.
  • Roll return denominator is the near price. Contango (far > near) gives a negative roll.
  • Collateral return must be pro-rated for the holding period (3 months = annual rate × 0.25). Total return = price + roll + collateral, all for the same period.
  • Rolling: sell exposure/near price contracts and buy exposure/far price contracts. Cheaper far contract → buy more.
  • Convenience yield is high when inventories are scarce. It is subtracted in the storage formula.
  • Insurance theory → always backwardation. Contango where consumers hedge more = hedging pressure.
  • Negative roll ≠ worse total return: price and collateral returns can more than offset it.
  • Swap direction: total return swap buyer pays when the index falls. To bet on lower volatility, go short a volatility swap. To bet on the index rising, go long an excess return swap. To bet on one grade beating another, use a basis swap.
  • Production-weighted index (S&P GSCI) is more sensitive to energy than an equal-weighted index.
  • Indexes on several exchanges do not add much diversification (exchanges are highly correlated).
