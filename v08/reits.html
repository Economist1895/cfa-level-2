Investments in Real Estate through Publicly Traded Securities

In 30 seconds

  • Public real estate = REITs (tax-exempt pass-throughs, must pay out 90–100%), REOCs (ordinary taxable, flexible) and MBS.
  • NAVPS = (market value of assets − market value of liabilities) / shares. Property value = next-12-month cash NOI ÷ cap rate.
  • FFO = NI + depreciation & amortisation − gains on property sales. AFFO = FFO − non-cash rent − recurring capex → best proxy for dividend capacity.
  • Value with P/FFO, P/AFFO (driven by growth, property risk, leverage), EV/EBITDA, or a 2–3 stage DDM/DCF.
  • Public vs private: liquidity, low minimums and professional management vs higher volatility and stock-market correlation in the short run.

01Types & REIT structure

  • Equity REITs own property. Mortgage REITs hold real-estate loans. Tax advantage: dividends are deductible (or REIT is simply exempt) → single taxation.
  • REOC chosen when: no REIT regime in the country, heavy for-sale development, or non-qualifying services (brokerage, third-party management).
  • MBS: RMBS (thousands of loans) and CMBS (≈100 loans down to one). Real estate debt markets dwarf listed equity.
  • REITs dominate in North America; REOCs are more common in Europe and Asia Pacific.

Typical REIT tests

  • Distribute 90–100% of taxable income. ≥ 75% of assets in real estate. ≥ 75% of income from rents or mortgage interest.
  • US: ≥ 100 shareholders and the 5/50 rule (5 or fewer holders can't own > 50%). Limits on development, leverage, concentration.
  • Self-managed (internally advised) REITs have fewer conflicts. External managers paid on assets have an incentive to grow size.
  • Non-qualifying activities go in a taxable REIT subsidiary (TRS).

REIT advantages

  • Liquidity, transparency
  • Diversified property holdings
  • Professional management
  • Stable income, high yield
  • Tax efficiency

REIT disadvantages

  • No retained earnings → must raise capital to grow
  • Public-company compliance costs
  • Prices move with stocks → less portfolio diversification
  • Limited activities

02Net asset value approach

  • IFRS: investment property at cost or fair value (fair value changes go through NI; applied to all investment property). US GAAP: historical cost → understates appreciated assets, so book value isn't useful.
F1

NAV per share

NAVPS=SharesMVassets​−MVliabilities​​

The primary metric in Europe and Asia (US analysts favour P/FFO). Calculated before any tax on liquidation.

F2

Building NAV from NOI

NOIValue​=Rent−Vacancy−OpEx=Cap rateNext-12m cash NOI​​

Steps: trailing NOI − non-cash (straight-line) rent + full-year effect of acquisitions = cash NOI; grow it one year; capitalise. Add cash, receivables, land (at book or market), prepaid. Exclude goodwill, deferred financing costs, deferred tax assets. Subtract debt at market value and other liabilities. Ignore deferred tax liabilities.

  • Cap rate comes from comparable transactions. Higher cap rate → lower NAV. Rising rates → buyers demand higher cap rates → NAV falls.
  • Other inputs: value per ft²/unit, or disclosed appraisals (adjust if you disagree).
  • Land bought long ago should be marked up to today's value. The operating cap rate does not apply to non-income land.
  • Price below NAV → possible undervaluation, but also harder to raise equity (issuing below NAV is dilutive) → slower growth.
  • Premiums/discounts of 25%+ occur. Premium justified by management that adds value, public liquidity (lower required return), better access to capital.
  • NAV treats the REIT as a static pool, which ignores going-concern value. It becomes subjective when transactions dry up. Often best used relatively (e.g. smallest premium, implied cap rate from the share price).

03FFO, AFFO & multiples

F3

Funds from operations

FFO​=NI+Depr.+Amort.−Gains on property sales(+Losses)​

Real estate tends to hold its value, so depreciation isn't economic. Property sale gains aren't recurring. Full definition also excludes deferred tax charges, debt-restructuring gains/losses and impairments. FFO is not cash flow.

F4

Adjusted FFO (FAD / CAD)

AFFO​=FFO−Non-cash rent−Recurring capex​
Non-cash rent
Straight-line rent − cash rent (positive in early lease years with step-ups)
Recurring capex
Maintenance, leasing commissions, tenant improvements

Best measure of economic income and dividend-paying capacity, but less standardised and harder to estimate → data providers track FFO and NAV, rarely AFFO.

F5

Relative-value price

P=FFO/share×Peer P/FFOP=AFFO/share×Peer P/AFFO​

P/FFO ≈ the REIT version of P/E. P/AFFO ≈ a cash-flow multiple. EBITDA/EV ≈ the property cap rate and is unaffected by leverage.

Multiple driverHigher multiple when…
Growth in FFO/AFFODevelopment success, supply-constrained prime markets, skilled management
Property riskStable cash flows (apartments > hotels), young well-kept assets
Capital structureLower leverage. FFO/AFFO are levered, so higher debt → lower multiples (and stock overhang)

P/FFO, P/AFFO pros

  • Widely accepted, comparable across sectors
  • FFO estimates readily available
  • Combine with growth & leverage

Cons

  • Miss value of non-income assets (land, vacant, development), under-used assets, below-market rents
  • P/FFO ignores recurring capex; AFFO estimates vary
  • One-offs and accounting changes distort

04DDM / DCF & choosing a method

F6

Two-step dividend discount model

V0​=t=1∑n​(1+r)tDt​​+(1+r)nPn​​Pn​=r−gDn+1​​​

High payouts make DDM/DCF natural. r often from CAPM. Typical: 2–3 years of explicit dividends, then a constant-growth terminal value. Lower long-term growth (e.g. new supply allowed by zoning) → lower value.

  • Methods give different answers → re-examine assumptions. Choose by reliability of inputs and what other investors use.
  • In a downturn with few transactions, NAV is the least useful method. A defensive REIT may keep relatively higher multiples than peers even as all multiples fall.
  • A REIT holding land that peers lack deserves a higher multiple (value not in FFO).
  • Same P/NAV, lower cap rate in its NAV → less NOI per unit of value → lower FFO → higher P/FFO.
  • Property-type risks: self-storage = easy entry → overbuilding. Health care = recession-resistant but exposed to demographics and government funding.

05Public vs private real estate

Private (direct)Public (REITs, REOCs)
ProsControl, low volatility, low correlation, illiquidity premium, any strategy (e.g. merchant development), tax benefitsLiquidity, low minimums and entry/exit costs, professional management, diversified access (data centres, storage), no double tax
ConsIlliquid, high fees and minimums, appraisal lag, low transparency, returns often from leverageHigher volatility, short-term equity correlation, activity limits, may trade below NAV, dividends taxed as income
  • Most real-estate expertise needed: direct single property. Most flexibility: REOC. Best for liquidity: REIT/REOC. Best for growth: REOC or direct.
  • Diversification: pick a mix of property types and markets (e.g. office + retail, urban + suburban), not one type in one city.
  • Public markets let investors act on short-term views. Wide, persistent discounts to NAV can lead to take-privates.

06Exam traps

⚠️
  • NAVPS: capitalise NOI, add other tangible assets, then deduct debt. Book assets/share or property value/share alone are distractors.
  • Use next-12-month cash NOI: strip straight-line rent and grow one year before dividing by the cap rate.
  • Deferred tax liabilities are ignored ("soft"), so they don't reduce NAV. Goodwill and deferred tax assets are excluded.
  • FFO from AFFO: add back non-cash rent and recurring capex. Don't add depreciation again (it's already in FFO).
  • FFO: subtract gains, add losses on property sales. Add back depreciation and amortisation only.
  • AFFO > FFO as a measure of economic return and dividend capacity. Both are levered measures.
  • Higher leverage → lower P/FFO. Higher cap rate → lower NAV.
  • A discount to NAV can signal undervaluation and restrict equity raising and growth.
  • Self-managed REITs have fewer conflicts than externally managed ones.
  • DDM: terminal value uses Dn+1/(r − g) and is discounted n periods, together with Dn.
