Currency Exchange Rates: Equilibrium Value

In 30 seconds

  • Quotes are P/B: price of 1 unit of the base (denominator) currency. Bid/offer always refer to the base currency.
  • Covered interest parity is the only parity enforced by arbitrage; it sets forward points. The others hold only in the long run, if at all.
  • Ideal world: inflation gap = interest gap = forward premium = expected spot change, and real rates are equal everywhere.
  • UIP fails short term → the carry trade earns money, with crash risk (negative skew, fat tails).
  • Short run: capital flows and policy mix dominate (Mundell–Fleming). Long run: PPP, current account, debt sustainability.

01Quotes, spreads & triangular arbitrage

  • Bid = price at which the dealer buys the base; offer = dealer sells the base. Offer > bid, always.
  • Client sells base → hits the bid; client buys base → pays the offer.
  • Spot settles T + 2 (CAD/USD is T + 1). Anything longer is a forward.
  • A pip = 0.0001 (0.01 for JPY quotes).

What widens the dealer's spread

  • Interbank liquidity: obscure pair, off-hours (best when London & New York overlap, ~8–11 a.m. NY), high volatility.
  • Large size (harder to lay off) or tiny retail size.
  • Weak relationship with the client; credit risk matters only a little at spot (short settlement).
  • Forwards only: longer term → wider (less liquidity, more counterparty and interest-rate risk).

No-arbitrage constraints

  • A dealer's bid can't exceed the interbank offer; its offer can't be below the interbank bid.
  • Same for a cross rate vs the implied interbank cross. If the dealer's bid > implied offer: buy base in the interbank market, sell to the dealer. If the dealer's offer < implied bid: buy from the dealer, sell in the interbank market.
  • Quotes that merely overlap (dealer 79.82/79.87 vs implied 79.84/79.86) → no arbitrage.

Base currency at a forward premium ⇔ iP​>iB​. Higher-yield currency trades at a forward discount. Size scales with spot, the rate gap and (roughly) time.

Forward points

  • All-in forward = spot + points/10,000 (points/100 for JPY). Points are not annualised.
  • Use the same side for spot and points: selling base → spot bid + bid points; buying base → spot offer + offer points.
  • Negative points: the bid is the more negative number (−25.4/−24.6).
  • Positive points → base at a premium → base-currency rate is lower than the price-currency rate.

Steps: (1) offsetting forward for the same size and settlement date; (2) price it on the correct side (selling base → bid; buying base → offer) using points for the remaining term; (3) settlement-date cash flow in the price currency; (4) discount at the price-currency rate for the remaining term. Formula is for long base; short base: flip the sign.

Trap: if the contract size is in the price currency (e.g. sold USD 10m in USD/NZD), convert: cash flow in NZD = 10m/Forig − 10m/Fclose, then discount at the NZD rate. Discount currency = currency of the cash flow.

04The carry trade

Borrow the low-yield (funding) currency, invest in the high-yield one. Profits only because UIP fails in the short/medium term.

F10

S0​,S1​
funding ccy per 1 unit of high-yield ccy (high-yield = base), today and at the end. If quoted the other way, use S0​/S1​.

Build cross rates for today and one year later first. Convert out at S0​, earn ihigh​, convert back at S1​, then subtract the borrowing rate.

  • Returns: peaked, fat tails, negative skew → crash risk when volatility spikes and positions unwind.
  • Volatility of return on equity comes from leverage.
  • Works in calm, low-volatility regimes; safe-haven demand in a crisis hurts it.

05Balance of payments & capital flows

  • Current account deficit = capital account surplus. Persistent deficits → depreciation over the long run; surpluses → appreciation.
  • Short run, capital flows dominate: asset prices adjust fast, goods prices and trade volumes slowly; portfolio stocks are huge vs trade flows.

Three current account channels

  • Flow supply/demand: surplus → demand for the currency. Adjustment size depends on the initial trade gap, price pass-through and demand elasticity. Long lags, so not an immediate correction.
  • Portfolio balance: wealth shifts to surplus nations; if they rebalance away from the deficit country's assets, its currency weakens.
  • Debt sustainability: rising external debt → markets revise the deficit currency's long-run equilibrium value down (surplus currency up).

Capital flows

  • Tighter money, structural reform, lower deficits → lower risk premium, capital inflows, appreciation; effect of yield spreads is gradual.
  • Inflow surges → booms, bubbles, overvaluation, then sudden reversal.
  • Equity–currency correlation is unstable and ≈ 0 in the long run. Safe-haven currencies (e.g. USD) rise in "risk-off".
  • Higher expected inflation gap → EM currency depreciates. Safe-haven demand → higher EM risk premium.

06Monetary & fiscal policy

Mundell–Fleming (short run, demand side, no price change)

  • Easy money → lower rates → capital outflow → depreciation.
  • Expansionary fiscal → higher rates → inflow (+) but bigger trade deficit (−). With mobile capital, the inflow wins → appreciation.

High mobility = developed (G-10) markets, via capital flows. Low mobility = EMs with controls, via the trade balance.

Monetary models (output fixed, prices move)

  • Pure monetary: X% more money → X% higher prices → X% depreciation via PPP. Weakness: assumes PPP holds short and long run.
  • Dornbusch overshooting: prices sticky short run → easier money cuts rates → outflow → currency overshoots below its long-run PPP level, then partly recovers.
  • Focus: inflation and the price level (not risk premiums).

Portfolio balance (long run, fiscal)

  • Sustained deficits add bonds; investors hold them only for a higher expected return: higher rates/risk premium, and/or an immediate depreciation (to expect later gains).
  • Combined view: fiscal expansion with mobile capital → appreciation short run, then depreciation long run as debt mounts (fear of debt monetisation, or forced fiscal tightening).

07Intervention, controls & crises

  • Pull factors (domestic: lower inflation, flexible FX, better fiscal, privatisation, liberalisation) vs push factors (low rates in developed markets, asset allocation trends).
  • To stop unwanted appreciation: capital controls or selling own currency. IMF now accepts controls as a legitimate tool.
  • To defend a peg while easing: buy own currency.
  • DM intervention: limited effect (tiny vs FX turnover). EM: more effective, because FX reserves are large relative to turnover. Lowers volatility more than it shifts levels.

Warning signs of a currency crisis

  • Liberalised capital markets; big inflows, esp. short-term foreign-currency debt.
  • Banking crisis before/alongside; fixed or partially fixed exchange rate.
  • FX reserves fall sharply; currency well above its historical mean.
  • Terms of trade deteriorate; broad money growth and M2/reserves rise; higher inflation.

Good early-warning system: predicts crises, few false alarms, signals early, uses timely (not lagged) data. Crises usually surprise markets and ratings agencies; even sound economies can be hit by sentiment shifts.

08Exam traps

⚠️
  • Buying the base currency → pay the offer. Converting JPY into GBP with JPY/GBP → divide by the offer.
  • Forward premium formula: the denominator uses the base rate iB​; sign comes from iP​−iB​.
  • MTM: close out over the remaining term (6 months into a 9-month forward → 3-month points), discount at the price-currency rate.
  • Positive GBP/EUR points → EUR at a premium → EUR rate < GBP rate.
  • Fully hedged foreign deposit earns the domestic rate (CIP). No calculation needed.
  • Carry trades profit because UIP fails, not CIP or IFE.
  • Forward = unbiased forecast needs CIP + UIP; real rate parity is not required.
  • Flow channel: trade imbalances do not correct immediately; long lags.
  • Risk premiums belong to the portfolio balance model, not monetary models.
  • Before a crisis, FX reserves fall (they don't rise). Low mobility + both policies restrictive → appreciation.
