Valuation of Contingent Claims

In 30 seconds

  • An option = a replicating portfolio of the underlying + borrowing/lending. A call = leveraged long stock. A put = short stock + lending.
  • Binomial: value = PV at the risk-free rate of the expected payoff under the risk-neutral probability π. American options: check early exercise at every node.
  • BSM: c = stock component − bond component. N(d₁) drives delta; N(d₂) = RN probability a call ends in the money.
  • Black model = BSM on a futures/forward price. Adapted for caps/floors (underlying = FRA rate) and swaptions (underlying = forward swap rate).
  • Greeks: delta (hedge ratio), gamma (curvature, jump risk), theta (time decay), vega (volatility, the biggest input), rho (rates).

01One-period binomial model

  • Assumptions: replicating instruments investable, no frictions, short selling allowed, known distribution of the underlying, borrow/lend at rf.
  • u = S⁺/S and d = S⁻/S are total returns (1 + return). Higher volatility → higher u, lower d.
  • At expiry: cT = Max(0, ST − X), pT = Max(0, X − ST).

Call = buy h shares, borrow PV(hS⁻ − c⁻). Put = short |h| shares, lend. Writers do the reverse.

F3

Expectations approach

π=u−d(1+r)−d​c=1+rπc++(1−π)c−​​

Same answer as F2, usually quicker. π is the risk-neutral probability: objective, not the investor's view, and it doesn't depend on X. Discount at the risk-free rate, not a risk-adjusted rate.

F4

Put–call parity

S+p=PV(X)+c

Holds under any model (European options, same X and T). Use it to get the put from the call.

💡
Mispriced option: market call > model → sell the call, buy h shares, borrow. Profit = the gap today, with zero cash flow at expiry. Call too cheap → buy it, short h shares, lend.

02Two-period trees, American options & rate trees

  • Two-period tree = three one-period trees. Work backward from expiry. The lattice recombines: S⁺⁻ = S⁻⁺ = udS.
  • Replication is dynamic (h changes at each node) and self-financing (no new money needed).
F5

Two-period European option

c=(1+r)2π2c+++2π(1−π)c+−+(1−π)2c−−​

Same for puts. Discount over two periods.

American options

  • At each node, value = max(model value, exercise value). Replace the model value when early exercise pays, then continue back. This changes both the value and the hedge ratio.
  • F5 (one-shot expectation) is wrong for American options when early exercise matters. Only the node-by-node approach works.
  • Calls on non-dividend stock: never exercised early (worth more alive), so American = European.
  • Puts: early exercise can pay (deep ITM: invest the proceeds at r). American put ≥ European put.
  • Dividends (escrow method: model S − PV(div)) can make early exercise of an American call optimal just before the ex-date.

Interest rate options on a binomial tree

  • The underlying is the spot rate at each node. A call pays when rate > exercise rate, a put when rate < exercise rate. Payoff = notional × Max(0, rate − X).
  • RN probability = 0.5. Discount one step at a time using that node's rate. The factors differ for the up and down nodes.

03Black–Scholes–Merton

  • Assumptions: geometric Brownian motion (log returns normal → prices lognormal), prices move continuously (no jumps), continuous trading, liquid underlying, short selling, no frictions, no arbitrage, European exercise, constant known r and σ, any yield is continuous and constant.
  • r and σ are annual and continuously compounded.

  • Short put = over-leveraged long stock (borrowing > 100% of the stock cost).

Currency options

  • S = domestic per 1 foreign. r = domestic rate; γ = foreign rate. S and X in the same units; σ = volatility of the FX rate.
  • A put on the foreign currency = a call on the domestic currency.

05The Greeks

Call delta in [0, e−δT]; put delta in [−e−δT, 0]. Stock delta = ±1. Delta is not the probability of ending ITM.

NH < 0 → sell/short the hedging instrument. Short calls → buy stock. Short puts → short stock. Long stock → sell calls (N = −shares/Δc).

  • Gamma: stock has gamma 0, so only options change portfolio gamma. Buying any option ↑ gamma. A delta-hedged short call is short gamma: large moves either way hurt. Fix gamma first, then delta.
  • Vega: volatility is the input that matters most. Low σ → option values fall to their lower bounds: Max(0, S − PV(X)) for a call, Max(0, PV(X) − S) for a put. Volatility can only be hedged with other options.
  • Rho: a call saves on financing the stock, so higher r → call ↑. With r = 0, ATM call = ATM put.

06Implied volatility

  • Implied volatility = the σ that makes the model price equal the market price (like YTM for bonds). It is forward-looking; historical σ looks back.
  • Options are quoted in vol: lets you compare options with different strikes and expiries, and revalue a book at market vols.
  • Model price (historical σ) > market price → implied vol is below historical vol.
  • Trade your view: think implied vol is too low → buy the option; too high → sell.
  • Volatility smile = IV vs exercise price (2-D). Volatility surface = IV vs exercise price and expiry (3-D). Under BSM it would be flat; in practice it isn't.
  • VIX ≈ 30-day implied vol of the S&P 500 ("fear index"). Rising IV = higher price of hedging.

07Exam traps

⚠️
  • π is risk-neutral: it comes from u, d and r only. Changing X doesn't change π (true for rate trees too). Discount at rf.
  • Two periods: square the one-period discount factor. Forgetting it is the classic distractor.
  • American put: at each node compare X − S with the model value and keep the higher, then roll back. Exercise value replaces the model value at that node.
  • Overpriced call → sell it, buy h shares, borrow PV(hS⁻ − c⁻). Arbitrage profit = market price − model value today.
  • Black / rate options: the underlying is the futures price or FRA rate, not spot or spot MRR. Rate options discount to the payment date (expiry + accrual period).
  • Swaptions: underlying = forward swap rate. T = swaption expiry. The current swap rate is not a discount rate.
  • Currency options: r = rate of the currency the price is quoted in (domestic); γ = foreign rate.
  • Delta hedge signs: short puts have positive delta → short stock. Hedging with calls: divide by the call delta (more contracts than shares).
  • Gamma: adding stock or futures can't change gamma. Long options (calls or puts) add positive gamma.
  • N(d₂) (not N(d₁)) = RN probability the call finishes ITM. Put components use N(−d₁) and N(−d₂) = 1 − N(·).
